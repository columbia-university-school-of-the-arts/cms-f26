# Mapping a corpus you did not write

A folder of readings is not yet a corpus. It becomes one when you can ask it a
question and check the answer.

This skill produces that map. Its shape is fixed so that two students can
compare theirs, and its rules exist because the failure it guards against is
not a wrong answer but a **fluent** one: a table with every cell filled, no
indication of which cells were read and which were guessed, and no way to tell
the difference without redoing the work.

## The table

One row per reading. Four columns:

| Column | Contents |
|---|---|
| Reading | Author surname and short title |
| Central claim | One sentence, in the author's terms rather than yours |
| Week | The week it is assigned, or `unknown` |
| Argues with | One other text in this corpus, and the point of contact |

## Three requirements

**1. Every claim carries its source.** After each central claim, name the file
it came from and give a quotation short enough to find by searching — a few
words, not a paragraph. A claim you cannot quote is a claim you inferred, and
it belongs in the section below instead.

**2. Declare what you could not determine.** End with a section headed
`## Could not determine`, listing every cell you guessed at or left `unknown`,
and why. Reasons worth distinguishing:

- the text does not say
- the file is unreadable or badly converted at that point
- the answer is not in the corpus at all (an assignment week, for instance,
  usually lives in the syllabus rather than in the reading)
- two passages conflict and choosing between them is a judgement

**Never fill a cell to avoid leaving it empty.** A declared gap is a result. A
plausible sentence covering a gap is a fabrication, and it costs the reader
their ability to trust any other row.

**3. Name what the conversion cost.** These readings are PDFs, and reading them
means extracting a text layer first. End with a section headed
`## What the conversion lost`, naming what is now missing or damaged — page
numbers, figures and their captions, tables, footnote anchors, columns run
together, characters garbled by OCR or by a bad encoding. Be specific: name
files, not categories.

## Rules

- Never present the table as *what these texts say*. Present it as what you
  could establish from these files, in this form, today.
- If a reading is named in the syllabus but absent from the folder, say so.
  A hole in the corpus is worth more to the reader than a silent omission.
- Prefer "unknown" over a hedge. "Possibly argues with Moretti" is a filled
  cell pretending to be an empty one.
